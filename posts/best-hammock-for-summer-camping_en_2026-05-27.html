What Actually Matters When Choosing

Fabric Weight and Denier

Nylon hammocks are rated by denier — the weight of the thread. 70D nylon is entry-level and shows wear faster. 210D or higher is bomber but heavier. For summer backpacking, 70D to 100D hits the sweet spot: light enough to carry, durable enough to not worry about snags on rough bark. Don't let marketing terms like "triple-stitched" substitute for actual denier specs.

Suspension System Quality

The hammock fabric rarely fails. The suspension does. Check what's included — bare carabiners and cord are not the same as rated aluminum wiregate carabiners with proper tree straps. Many hammocks ship without adequate tree straps, which means an extra purchase. Budget at least $15 for straps if they're not included, and look for 1-inch webbing that's at least 9 feet long to wrap trees properly without bark damage.

Weight Capacity vs. Your Actual Weight

Manufacturers list max capacities that assume ideal hang angles and no dynamic loading. If the tag says 400 lbs, treat 300 lbs as your real ceiling. Dynamic loading — bouncing, sitting down hard, rolling over in your sleep — spikes the load on hardware well above static weight. This matters most on budget gear where the safety margins are thinner.

Breathability for Hot Weather

Summer camping means warm nights. A hammock that's too tight in its weave or made of polyester blends traps heat underneath you and turns uncomfortable fast. Single-layer nylon is cooler than double-layer for July and August use. Save the double-layer for fall when you need the underquilt pocket. If you're camping south of the Mason-Dixon line in August, breathability isn't optional.

Bug Protection

This gets ignored until the first night you hang without a net in Georgia in June. Integrated bug nets add maybe 4 to 6 oz and save you from a genuinely miserable night. If your hammock doesn't include one and you're camping anywhere with standing water nearby, buy a separate hammock net or plan to use a tent instead. Summer camping east of the Rockies without bug protection is a choice you'll regret exactly once.

Frequently Asked Questions

What's the best hammock for hot summer nights?

Single-layer nylon hammocks breathe the best in warm weather. Skip the double-layer models for summer use — they're built around underquilt compatibility and hold heat you don't want. In temperatures above 65°F, you'll sleep better with airflow underneath you than with insulation. A simple 70D to 100D nylon single-layer with a loose diagonal hang keeps you cool without any added gear.

Do I need a bug net for hammock camping in summer?

Yes, if you're camping anywhere east of the Rockies or in the Southeast. Mosquitoes and no-see-ums are active from dusk to dawn through most of summer, and a hammock without a net leaves you completely exposed. Either buy a hammock with an integrated net or purchase a dedicated hammock net separately. It's one of those accessories that feels optional until the first time you actually need it.

How do I hang a hammock without damaging trees?

Use 1-inch nylon tree straps that are at least 9 feet long. Wrap them high on the trunk — at least head height — and keep the contact area wide to distribute pressure across the bark. Never use bare cord or rope directly against a tree; it cuts into the cambium layer and can kill the tree over time. Most hammock manufacturers sell compatible tree straps, and many hammock-specific straps are designed to meet Leave No Trace guidelines. The hang angle should be roughly 30 degrees from horizontal for comfort and structural integrity.
